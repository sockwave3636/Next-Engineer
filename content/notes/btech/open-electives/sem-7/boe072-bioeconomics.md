# BOE072 Bioeconomics: One-Day Exam Revision Notes

Original revision notes written for the AKTU syllabus. Every unit has the same parts:
1. **2-mark definitions**: short answers you can write word for word in Section A.
2. **Core concepts**: frameworks, models and comparison tables.
3. **Examples and comparisons**: worked cases and the small calculations that come up (Gordon-Schaefer fishery model, maximum sustainable yield, logistic growth, Pigouvian tax, biofuel energy and emission balance, value-chain margins, cascading use).
4. **Key points to remember**.
5. **Important questions**: the long questions to prepare, with what your answer must contain.

## How to use these notes the day before the paper

- **Morning (3 hours):** Unit 1 (definition, scope, history and principles of the bioeconomy, role of biological resources, sustainability, public and political discourse) and Unit 2 (growth, development, innovation, environmental economics, the role of government, modelling, the bio-based economy and sustainable development).
- **Afternoon (3 hours):** Unit 3 (interdisciplinary research, primary production and processing, markets, sustainability management and entrepreneurship) and Unit 4 (bio-based value chains, food security and nutrition, biomass for fuels and chemicals, biotechnology).

- **In the exam:** define terms precisely, draw the "food first" biomass value pyramid and the logistic growth curve, and give Indian examples (ethanol blending, SATAT, National Biomass Mission, BioE3 policy).

Section C has 5 questions (one per unit), each with two parts, attempt one part of each, 7 marks each (35). Every unit gets its own Section C question, so do not skip any unit.

## Unit 1: Introduction to Bioeconomy

### 2-mark definitions

- **Bioeconomy:** the production, use and conservation of biological resources (plants, animals, microorganisms, biomass, and related knowledge and technology) to provide products, processes and services across all economic sectors within a sustainable economic system (definition adopted by the Global Bioeconomy Summit 2018).
- **Bioeconomics:** (1) the study of the economics of biological resources, such as fisheries, forests and agriculture, combining population biology with economics; (2) more broadly, the economic analysis of the bioeconomy.
- **Biological resources:** renewable living resources and their products: crops, forests, livestock, fish, algae, microorganisms, genetic resources and organic wastes.
- **Biomass:** organic material of biological origin (excluding fossil material) used as food, feed, materials or energy.
- **Bio-based product:** a product wholly or partly derived from biomass, such as bioplastics, bio-lubricants and bio-chemicals.
- **Circular bioeconomy:** a bioeconomy that keeps materials in use through reuse, recycling and cascading, and returns nutrients to the soil.
- **Sustainability (Brundtland, 1987):** development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- **Ecosystem services:** benefits people obtain from ecosystems: provisioning (food, timber), regulating (climate, water purification), supporting (soil formation, nutrient cycling) and cultural (recreation).

### Core concepts

**Scope of the bioeconomy:** agriculture, forestry, fisheries and aquaculture; food and beverages; pulp and paper; wood products; bio-based chemicals and plastics; pharmaceuticals and biotechnology; bioenergy (biofuels, biogas); textiles from natural fibres; waste and residue valorization; ecosystem services.

**History of the bioeconomy**

| Period | Development |
|---|---|
| Pre-industrial | Economies were essentially bio-based: food, wood, fibres, animal power |
| 1970s | Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen used "bioeconomics" to link economics with biological and thermodynamic limits; Colin Clark's "Mathematical Bioeconomics" (1976) on renewable resources |
| 1990s–2000s | Biotechnology revolution; OECD "The Bioeconomy to 2030" (2009) |
| 2012 | EU Bioeconomy Strategy; US National Bioeconomy Blueprint |
| 2015 onward | Global Bioeconomy Summits; Sustainable Development Goals; circular bioeconomy |
| India | National Biotechnology Development Strategy, BIRAC (2012), ethanol blending programme, BioE3 policy (2024: Biotechnology for Economy, Environment and Employment) |

**Core principles:** use renewable biological resources in place of fossil ones; food security first; sustainability (environmental, social, economic); resource efficiency and cascading use; circularity; knowledge and innovation-based (biotechnology); conservation of biodiversity; inclusiveness (rural development).

**Three visions of the bioeconomy (Bugge et al.)**

| Vision | Focus |
|---|---|
| Bio-technology vision | Research, biotech innovation, commercialization |
| Bio-resource vision | Processing and upgrading biomass, new value chains |
| Bio-ecology vision | Sustainability, biodiversity, ecosystems, local circular systems |

**Importance for sustainability:** reduces greenhouse gas emissions and fossil dependence, creates rural jobs and income, adds value to residues and wastes, supports climate adaptation, contributes to SDGs (2 zero hunger, 7 clean energy, 12 responsible consumption, 13 climate action, 15 life on land).

**Public and political discourse:** debates over food vs fuel, land use change and deforestation, GMOs and gene editing, biodiversity loss, fair benefit sharing with farmers and indigenous communities (Nagoya Protocol), "greenwashing", and whether the bioeconomy should prioritize growth or sufficiency. Bioeconomy strategies influence policy agendas, research funding and public acceptance.

### Examples and comparisons

**Example 1: Fossil-based vs bio-based economy.**

| Feature | Fossil-based | Bio-based |
|---|---|---|
| Resource | Coal, oil, gas (finite) | Biomass (renewable) |
| Carbon | Adds new CO₂ to atmosphere | Recycles recent atmospheric CO₂ |
| Location | Concentrated reserves | Distributed, rural |
| Waste | Often non-biodegradable | Often biodegradable or recyclable |
| Limits | Depletion, climate change | Land, water, biodiversity, food competition |

**Example 2: Indian bioeconomy.** India's bioeconomy was valued at about US$ 137 billion in 2022 and the government target is US$ 300 billion by 2030 (DBT/BIRAC India Bioeconomy Reports). Components: biopharma (vaccines), bio-agri (Bt cotton, biofertilizers), bio-industrial (enzymes, biofuels), bio-IT and research services.

### Key points to remember

- Bioeconomy = production, use and conservation of biological resources sustainably.
- Bioeconomics roots: Georgescu-Roegen, Colin Clark.
- Scope: agriculture, forestry, fisheries, food, bio-based materials, biotech, bioenergy.
- Three visions: biotech, bio-resource, bio-ecology.
- Discourse: food vs fuel, land use, GMOs, benefit sharing.

### Important questions

1. Define bioeconomy and explain its scope and history.
2. Explain the core principles of the bioeconomy and the role of biological resources.
3. Explain the importance of the bioeconomy for sustainability.
4. Explain the influence of the bioeconomy on public and political discourse.

## Unit 2: Growth, Innovation and Economics of the Bioeconomy

### 2-mark definitions

- **Economic growth:** increase in the output of goods and services (GDP) over time.
- **Economic development:** improvement in people's well-being, including income, health, education and equality, not just output.
- **Innovation:** introducing new or improved products, processes, business models or organization that create value.
- **Environmental economics:** the branch of economics that studies the interaction between the economy and the environment, especially externalities and policy instruments.
- **Externality:** a cost or benefit of an activity that falls on third parties and is not reflected in the market price (pollution is a negative externality).
- **Pigouvian tax:** a tax equal to the marginal external damage, to make producers pay for negative externalities.
- **Public good:** a good that is non-excludable and non-rival (clean air, basic research).
- **Tragedy of the commons:** overuse of a shared resource when individuals act in self-interest (Hardin, 1968).
- **Maximum sustainable yield (MSY):** the largest harvest that can be taken from a renewable population indefinitely.
- **Bio-based economy:** the part of the economy that uses biomass as a feedstock for materials, chemicals and energy, replacing fossil inputs.

### Core concepts

**Growth and development in the bioeconomy:** growth comes from new bio-based products and markets, higher farm productivity, valorization of residues and exports; development comes from rural jobs, better incomes, improved nutrition and health, and environmental quality. Growth must stay within planetary boundaries ("green growth" vs "degrowth" debate).

**Innovation in the bioeconomy:** biotechnology (enzymes, fermentation, genetic engineering, CRISPR), biorefineries, synthetic biology, precision agriculture, digital tools, new business models (circular, sharing, product-as-service). Innovation systems involve universities, firms, startups, government (funding, regulation) and users (quadruple helix).

**Environmental economics and the role of government**

| Market failure | Government response |
|---|---|
| Negative externalities (pollution, GHG) | Pigouvian taxes, carbon pricing, emission standards, cap-and-trade |
| Positive externalities (R&D, ecosystem services) | Subsidies, research funding, payment for ecosystem services |
| Public goods | Public provision and funding |
| Open-access resources | Property rights, quotas, licences, community management |
| Information gaps | Labels, certification (FSC, organic) |

Other roles: setting standards and blending mandates (ethanol in petrol), public procurement of bio-based products, infrastructure, skill development, risk sharing for first plants.

**Modelling tools**

- **Logistic growth:** dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K), where r is intrinsic growth rate and K carrying capacity. Maximum growth (and MSY) at N = K/2, MSY = rK/4.
- **Gordon-Schaefer fishery model:** harvest H = qEN (q catchability, E effort). Open access drives effort until profit is zero (bionomic equilibrium), leading to overfishing; maximum economic yield (MEY) uses less effort than MSY.
- **Life cycle assessment (LCA):** measures environmental impacts of a product from cradle to grave.
- **Input-output and computable general equilibrium (CGE) models:** study economy-wide effects of bioeconomy policies.
- **Cost-benefit analysis, system dynamics and agent-based models.**

**Bio-based economy and sustainable development:** biomass replaces fossil carbon in materials and energy; must meet economic (competitive), environmental (lower emissions, no biodiversity loss) and social (food security, fair income) dimensions. Tools: sustainability certification (RSB, ISCC), LCA, indicators linked to SDGs.

### Examples and comparisons

**Example 1: Logistic growth and MSY.** A fish stock has K = 10,000 tonnes and r = 0.4 per year.
- MSY occurs at N = K/2 = 5,000 tonnes.
- MSY = rK/4 = 0.4 × 10,000 / 4 = **1,000 tonnes per year**.
- If the stock is at 8,000 tonnes, growth = 0.4 × 8,000 × (1 − 0.8) = 640 tonnes per year, so harvesting more than 640 t would shrink the stock.

**Example 2: Pigouvian tax.** A sugar mill's effluent causes ₹50 of damage to downstream farmers for each tonne of cane crushed. A tax of ₹50 per tonne makes the mill include this cost, reducing output or encouraging treatment (for example converting effluent to biogas), until the marginal cost of treatment equals ₹50.

**Example 3: Tragedy of the commons.** Ten herders share a pasture that can feed 100 cattle. Each herder gains the full value of an extra cow but shares the cost of overgrazing with all; so each adds cattle until the pasture is ruined. Solutions: grazing quotas, private or community property rights, rotational grazing rules.

### Key points to remember

- Growth (output) vs development (well-being).
- Innovation: biotech, biorefineries, synthetic biology, digital agriculture.
- Market failures and government tools: taxes, subsidies, standards, quotas, mandates.
- Logistic model: MSY = rK/4 at N = K/2; open access leads to overexploitation.
- LCA, CGE and system dynamics as modelling tools.

### Important questions

1. Explain growth, development and innovation in the bioeconomy.
2. Explain environmental economics and the role of government in the bioeconomy.
3. Explain modelling tools in bioeconomics (logistic growth, Gordon-Schaefer, LCA).
4. Explain the bio-based economy and its relation to sustainable development.

## Unit 3: Research, Primary Production and Sustainability Management

### 2-mark definitions

- **Interdisciplinary research:** research that integrates methods and knowledge from several disciplines (biology, engineering, economics, social sciences) to solve complex problems.
- **Transdisciplinary research:** research that also involves non-academic stakeholders (farmers, industry, policymakers) in defining and solving problems.
- **Primary production:** producing raw biological resources: crops, livestock, forestry, fisheries and aquaculture.
- **Primary processing:** first-stage conversion of raw biomass into intermediate products, such as milling grain, crushing cane, sawing timber, extracting oil.
- **Biorefinery:** a facility that converts biomass into a spectrum of products (food, feed, chemicals, materials, energy), like an oil refinery but with biomass.
- **Sustainability management:** managing an organization's activities to improve its environmental, social and economic performance (triple bottom line).
- **Bio-entrepreneurship:** creating and running ventures based on biological resources or biotechnology.
- **Market:** an arrangement where buyers and sellers exchange goods; for bio-based products it includes commodity markets, niche green markets and government procurement.

### Core concepts

**Why bioeconomy research must be interdisciplinary:** biomass systems involve biology (yields, breeding), engineering (processing), economics (costs, markets), environmental science (impacts), social science (acceptance, livelihoods) and law (regulation, IP). Approaches: systems thinking, LCA, techno-economic analysis, participatory research, living labs.

**Primary production of bio-based resources**

| Sector | Resources | Sustainability issues |
|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | Food crops, oilseeds, sugarcane, fibres, residues | Soil health, water use, fertilizer and pesticide use |
| Forestry | Timber, pulpwood, non-timber forest products | Deforestation, biodiversity, community rights |
| Livestock | Milk, meat, hides, manure | Methane emissions, feed, land |
| Fisheries and aquaculture | Fish, shellfish, seaweeds | Overfishing, pollution, mangrove loss |
| Algae and microbes | Microalgae, fermentation organisms | Energy for cultivation, scale-up cost |

**Processing:** mechanical (milling, pressing), thermal (drying, pyrolysis), chemical (hydrolysis, transesterification), biological (fermentation, anaerobic digestion, enzymes). Biorefinery types: first-generation (sugar, starch, oil crops), second-generation (lignocellulose: straw, bagasse, wood), third-generation (algae), integrated (sugar mill producing sugar, ethanol, electricity from bagasse and fertilizer from press mud).

**Markets for bio-based products:** commodity markets (sugar, ethanol, palm oil), price competition with fossil products (oil price matters), premium markets (organic, eco-labels), policy-driven markets (blending mandates, green procurement), carbon markets. Barriers: higher costs, scale, consumer awareness, feedstock price volatility.

**Sustainability management tools:** environmental management systems (ISO 14001), LCA (ISO 14040/44), carbon footprint, certification (FSC for forests, RSPO for palm oil, Bonsucro for sugarcane, ISCC for biofuels), sustainability reporting (GRI, BRSR in India), triple bottom line (people, planet, profit).

**Entrepreneurship in the bioeconomy:** opportunities: biofertilizers and biopesticides, mushroom farming, bioplastics, natural dyes, biogas and bio-CNG, agri-waste to products (pellets, packaging), functional foods, enzymes; support: BIRAC BIG grants, bio-incubators, Startup India, FPOs. Challenges: long R&D cycles, regulatory approvals, access to capital, scale-up.

### Examples and comparisons

**Example 1: Integrated sugarcane biorefinery.** 100 tonnes of cane gives about 10–11 t sugar, about 4 t molasses (fermented to about 1 kL ethanol), about 30 t bagasse (burnt for steam and power, surplus electricity sold to the grid), about 3–4 t press mud (compost or bio-CNG). One feedstock yields food, fuel, power and fertilizer, which improves income and reduces waste.

**Example 2: Interdisciplinary project (paddy straw).** Problem: stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana causes air pollution in north India. Agronomy (straw management machines like Happy Seeder), engineering (pelletizing, bio-CNG plants), economics (cost to farmers, logistics), policy (subsidies, bans), sociology (farmer behaviour), health science (pollution impacts). Solutions need all of them.

### Key points to remember

- Interdisciplinary and transdisciplinary research.
- Primary production sectors and their issues.
- Biorefinery generations: 1G food crops, 2G lignocellulose, 3G algae.
- Markets: commodity, premium, policy-driven, carbon.
- Sustainability tools: ISO 14001, LCA, certification, triple bottom line.

### Important questions

1. Explain the need for interdisciplinary research in the bioeconomy.
2. Explain the primary production and processing of bio-based resources.
3. Explain the markets for bio-based products.
4. Explain sustainability management and entrepreneurship in the bioeconomy.

## Unit 4: Value Chains, Food Security and Biomass Use

### 2-mark definitions

- **Value chain:** the full range of activities that bring a product from raw material to the final consumer, with value added at each stage.
- **Bio-based value chain:** a value chain that starts with biomass, such as cane to sugar to ethanol, or wood to pulp to paper.
- **Food security (FAO, 1996):** exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs for an active and healthy life.
- **Four pillars of food security:** availability, access, utilization and stability.
- **Malnutrition:** undernutrition (stunting, wasting, underweight), micronutrient deficiencies (hidden hunger) and overweight/obesity.
- **Biofuel:** fuel produced from biomass: bioethanol, biodiesel, biogas/bio-CNG, sustainable aviation fuel.
- **Platform chemical:** a basic chemical from which many products can be made, such as ethanol, lactic acid, succinic acid, furfural.
- **Biotechnology:** the use of living organisms, cells or their components to make products or improve processes.
- **Red, green, white and blue biotechnology:** medical, agricultural, industrial and marine biotechnology.

### Core concepts

**Stages of a bio-based value chain:** biomass production → harvesting and collection → logistics and storage → pre-treatment → conversion (biorefinery) → product manufacturing → distribution and marketing → use → end of life (recycling, composting, energy recovery). Value-chain analysis looks at actors, margins, bottlenecks and sustainability at each step.

**Markets and entrepreneurship in value chains:** farmer producer organizations (FPOs) improve bargaining power; contract farming; e-NAM; value addition at village level (processing units) keeps more value with farmers.

**Food security and nutrition**

- Availability: production, stocks, imports. Access: income, prices, public distribution (PDS, National Food Security Act 2013). Utilization: diet diversity, clean water, health. Stability: protection from shocks (droughts, prices, pandemics).
- Bioeconomy contributions: higher yields (improved seeds, biofertilizers), biofortified crops (iron-rich pearl millet, zinc wheat), reduced post-harvest losses, alternative proteins (algae, insects, fermentation), using food waste.
- Risks: biofuel crops competing with food for land and water ("food vs fuel" debate, 2007–08 food price spike).

**Biomass for fuels and chemicals**

| Product | Feedstock | Process |
|---|---|---|
| First-generation ethanol | Sugarcane juice, molasses, grains | Fermentation, distillation |
| Second-generation ethanol | Rice straw, bagasse, corn stover | Pre-treatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation (Panipat 2G plant) |
| Biodiesel | Vegetable oils, used cooking oil, jatropha | Transesterification |
| Biogas and bio-CNG | Manure, agri-residue, municipal organic waste | Anaerobic digestion, upgrading (SATAT scheme) |
| Bioplastics | Starch, sugar (PLA), microbial PHA | Fermentation and polymerization |
| Bio-chemicals | Sugars, lignin, oils | Fermentation, catalysis |
| Solid biofuels | Wood, residues | Pelletizing, briquetting, torrefaction |

**Role of biotechnology:** improved crops (Bt cotton, drought tolerance, gene editing), microbial fermentation for chemicals and enzymes, biofertilizers and biopesticides, waste treatment (bioremediation), vaccines and biopharmaceuticals, industrial enzymes for 2G ethanol and textiles.

### Examples and comparisons

**Example 1: Value chain margins (illustrative).** Farmer sells 1 tonne of tomatoes at ₹8,000; trader sells to wholesaler at ₹11,000; wholesaler to retailer at ₹14,000; retail price ₹20,000. Farmer's share = 8,000 / 20,000 = **40%**. If an FPO processes the tomatoes into puree and sells directly at ₹14,000 worth of output (after ₹3,000 processing cost), farmers capture ₹11,000 instead of ₹8,000, a 37.5% gain.

**Example 2: Ethanol blending.** India's 20% ethanol blending target (E20) for petrol was reached in 2025. If petrol demand is 40 billion litres a year, E20 needs 0.2 × 40 = 8 billion litres of ethanol, which saves foreign exchange on crude and supports cane and grain farmers but raises questions about water use (sugarcane) and food grains.

**Example 3: Emission saving.** If petrol emits 2.3 kg CO₂ per litre and ethanol's life-cycle emissions are 60% lower per unit of energy, then replacing 1 billion litres of petrol energy with ethanol saves about 0.6 × 2.3 = 1.38 million tonnes of CO₂ (ignoring land-use change, which can reduce or even reverse the saving).

### Key points to remember

- Value chain stages from biomass to end of life; actors and margins.
- Food security pillars: availability, access, utilization, stability.
- Biofuels: 1G (food crops), 2G (residues), 3G (algae); biodiesel by transesterification; bio-CNG.
- Biotechnology colours: red, green, white, blue.
- Food vs fuel debate.

### Important questions

1. Explain bio-based value chains and processing, with an example.
2. Explain markets and entrepreneurship in bio-based value chains.
3. Explain food security and nutrition and the role of the bioeconomy.
4. Explain the use of biomass for fuels and chemicals.
5. Explain the role of biotechnology in the bioeconomy.

## Unit 5: Sustainable Biomass Use and Bioeconomy Policy

### 2-mark definitions

- **Cascading use of biomass:** using biomass first for the highest-value applications (food, materials) and then reusing or recycling it, with energy recovery last.
- **Food-first principle:** biomass should first serve food and feed needs before being used for fuels or materials.
- **Biomass value pyramid:** a hierarchy from high value, low volume uses (pharmaceuticals, fine chemicals) through food, feed, materials and chemicals, to low value, high volume uses (energy, fuels).
- **Indirect land use change (ILUC):** when bioenergy crops displace food crops, which then expand into forests or grasslands elsewhere, releasing carbon.
- **Payment for ecosystem services (PES):** paying land users to manage land in ways that provide ecosystem services, such as watershed protection or carbon storage.
- **Bioeconomy strategy:** a government policy framework setting goals and actions to develop the bioeconomy.
- **Fossil fuels:** coal, oil and natural gas formed from ancient biomass over millions of years; non-renewable.
- **Biofuel mandates:** laws requiring a minimum share of biofuel in transport fuel.

### Core concepts

**Sustainable use of biomass for different purposes**

| Use | Examples | Sustainability considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Food | Grains, fruits, vegetables, oils | Priority use; reduce waste |
| Feed | Fodder, oilcakes, distillers' grains (DDGS) | Co-products from biofuel plants can feed animals |
| Industrial products | Bioplastics, fibres, timber, chemicals | Store carbon; design for recycling |
| Bioenergy | Ethanol, biodiesel, biogas, pellets | Use residues and wastes; avoid deforestation |
| Ecosystem services | Forests for water, carbon, biodiversity | Not all biomass should be harvested |

**Principles for sustainable biomass use:** food first, cascading use, residues and wastes before dedicated crops, no conversion of high-carbon or high-biodiversity land, soil carbon maintenance (leave some residues), water efficiency, fair benefit sharing, certification and traceability.

**Use of biological knowledge:** traditional knowledge (medicinal plants, ayurveda, indigenous farming) and modern science; protection through the Biological Diversity Act 2002 and Nagoya Protocol (access and benefit sharing); Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) against biopiracy (neem, turmeric patents revoked).

**Bioeconomy in policy contexts**

| Country / region | Policy |
|---|---|
| European Union | Bioeconomy Strategy 2012, updated 2018 (circular, sustainable) |
| USA | National Bioeconomy Blueprint 2012; Executive Order on biotechnology and biomanufacturing 2022 |
| India | National Policy on Biofuels 2018 (amended 2022), Ethanol Blended Petrol programme, SATAT (bio-CNG), PM JI-VAN (2G ethanol), GOBARdhan, BioE3 policy 2024, National Mission on Natural Farming |
| Global | SDGs, Paris Agreement, Convention on Biological Diversity |

Policy instruments: research funding, mandates and blending targets, tax incentives, green public procurement, standards and labels, carbon pricing, bans (single-use plastics), skill programmes.

**Management of fossil fuels and biofuels**

| Fossil fuels | Biofuels |
|---|---|
| Finite reserves | Renewable if managed sustainably |
| Release ancient carbon | Release carbon recently absorbed (near carbon-neutral in theory) |
| High energy density, mature infrastructure | Lower energy density (ethanol about 2/3 of petrol per litre), need blending infrastructure |
| Import dependence for India (about 85% of crude) | Domestic production, rural income |
| Air pollution, climate change | Can cause land use change, water stress, food competition if poorly managed |

Transition strategy: improve efficiency, blend biofuels, electrify where possible, use advanced biofuels from residues, sustainable aviation fuel, carbon capture with bioenergy (BECCS) for negative emissions.

### Examples and comparisons

**Example 1: Cascading use of wood.** Timber → furniture (decades of carbon storage) → recycled particle board → paper or fibre products → energy recovery at end of life. Each step extracts more value than burning the log at once.

**Example 2: Energy content comparison.** Ethanol has about 21 MJ per litre and petrol about 32 MJ per litre. To replace the energy of 100 litres of petrol you need 100 × 32/21 ≈ **152 litres of ethanol**; this is why E20 vehicles show a small drop in mileage (about 6–7%).

**Example 3: ILUC effect.** A biodiesel crop saves 2 t CO₂ per hectare per year compared with diesel. If it causes forest clearing elsewhere that releases 100 t CO₂ per hectare, the "carbon payback time" is 100 / 2 = **50 years**, which shows why residues and degraded land are preferred.

### Key points to remember

- Food first; cascading use; biomass value pyramid.
- Residues and wastes before dedicated crops; avoid ILUC.
- Biological knowledge: traditional knowledge, ABS, Nagoya Protocol, TKDL.
- Indian policies: Biofuel Policy 2018, E20, SATAT, PM JI-VAN, GOBARdhan, BioE3.
- Fossil vs biofuel comparison; BECCS.

### Important questions

1. Explain the sustainable use of biomass for food, feed, industrial products, bioenergy and ecosystem services.
2. Explain cascading use and the biomass value pyramid.
3. Explain the bioeconomy in policy contexts, with Indian policies.
4. Compare fossil fuels and biofuels and explain their management.
