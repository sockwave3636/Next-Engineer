## Unit 1: Introduction to Viruses

### 2-mark definitions

- **Virus:** an obligate intracellular parasite made of a nucleic acid genome (DNA or RNA, never both) enclosed in a protein coat, which replicates only inside living host cells using the host's machinery.
- **Virion:** a complete, infectious virus particle outside the cell.
- **Capsid:** the protein shell around the viral genome, made of repeating subunits called **capsomeres**.
- **Nucleocapsid:** the capsid together with the enclosed genome.
- **Envelope:** a lipid bilayer derived from the host cell membrane surrounding some virions, carrying viral glycoprotein spikes (peplomers).
- **Bacteriophage:** a virus that infects bacteria (e.g. T4, lambda).
- **Viroid:** a small, circular, naked infectious RNA (about 250–400 nucleotides) without a protein coat, causing plant diseases (potato spindle tuber viroid).
- **Prion:** an infectious misfolded protein (PrPˢᶜ) without nucleic acid that causes transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (CJD, mad cow disease); not a virus.
- **Cytopathic effect (CPE):** visible changes in cultured cells caused by viral infection (rounding, lysis, syncytia, inclusion bodies).
- **Plaque:** a clear zone in a cell monolayer or bacterial lawn formed by lysis of cells by one infectious virus and its progeny; the basis of plaque assays (PFU).

### Core concepts

**History of virology**

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1796 | Edward Jenner uses cowpox to protect against smallpox (first vaccine) |
| 1885 | Louis Pasteur's rabies vaccine |
| 1892 | Dmitri Ivanovsky shows tobacco mosaic disease agent passes bacteria-proof filters |
| 1898 | Martinus Beijerinck calls it "contagium vivum fluidum" (living infectious fluid); Loeffler and Frosch find foot-and-mouth disease virus (first animal virus) |
| 1901 | Walter Reed: yellow fever virus (first human virus) spread by mosquitoes |
| 1915–17 | Twort and d'Herelle discover bacteriophages |
| 1935 | Wendell Stanley crystallizes TMV |
| 1939 | First electron micrograph of TMV |
| 1949 | Enders, Weller, Robbins grow poliovirus in cell culture |
| 1950s | Salk (inactivated) and Sabin (oral) polio vaccines |
| 1970 | Temin and Baltimore discover reverse transcriptase |
| 1980 | WHO declares smallpox eradicated |
| 1983 | HIV identified (Montagnier, Barré-Sinoussi) |
| 2003, 2012, 2019 | SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2 emerge |
| 2014 | India declared polio-free (with the WHO South-East Asia Region) |
| 2020 | First mRNA vaccines authorized |

**Properties of viruses:** acellular; contain only one type of nucleic acid; no ribosomes or energy-generating metabolism; replicate by assembly from components, not by binary fission; filterable (pass bacterial filters); not affected by antibiotics; can be crystallized.

**Diversity:** viruses infect all life forms: animals, plants, fungi, bacteria (phages), archaea, even other viruses (virophages). They are the most abundant biological entities (about 10³¹ phages on Earth).

**Shapes (symmetry)**

| Symmetry | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Helical | Capsomeres arranged in a helix around the genome; rod or filamentous | TMV, influenza and rabies nucleocapsids, Ebola |
| Icosahedral | 20 triangular faces, 12 vertices; efficient closed shell | Adenovirus, poliovirus, herpesvirus capsid |
| Complex | Neither purely helical nor icosahedral | Bacteriophage T4 (head, tail, tail fibres), poxviruses |
| Enveloped | Lipid envelope over helical or icosahedral nucleocapsid; pleomorphic | Influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2, herpes |

**Sizes:** 17–30 nm (circoviruses, parvoviruses ~20 nm, poliovirus ~30 nm) up to ~200–400 nm (poxviruses, visible by light microscopy at the limit); giant viruses (Mimivirus ~750 nm, Pandoravirus ~1 µm). Measured by electron microscopy, ultrafiltration and ultracentrifugation.

**Genome components:** DNA or RNA; single- or double-stranded; linear or circular; segmented or non-segmented; positive (mRNA-sense) or negative sense; size from ~1.7 kb (circovirus) to over 2 Mb (Pandoravirus). Genomes carry genes for structural proteins, replication enzymes (polymerases), regulatory and host-modulating proteins; special features: terminal repeats, IRES, cap, poly-A, packaging signals.

**Virus structure (draw it):** genome → capsid (capsomeres) → nucleocapsid → matrix protein (in some enveloped viruses) → envelope with glycoprotein spikes. Some virions carry enzymes (RNA polymerase in negative-strand viruses; reverse transcriptase in retroviruses).

**General replication cycle (6 steps):** (1) attachment (adsorption) to receptors, (2) penetration/entry (endocytosis, membrane fusion, injection), (3) uncoating, (4) synthesis of mRNA, proteins and genome (biosynthesis), (5) assembly (maturation), (6) release (lysis or budding).

**Isolation of viruses**
- **Cell (tissue) culture:** primary cells, diploid cell lines (WI-38, MRC-5), continuous cell lines (Vero, HeLa, MDCK); detect growth by CPE, haemadsorption, immunofluorescence.
- **Embryonated chicken eggs:** inoculation of chorioallantoic membrane (pocks, poxviruses), amniotic and allantoic cavities (influenza, used for vaccine production), yolk sac.
- **Laboratory animals:** suckling mice (arboviruses, coxsackie), when culture is not possible.
- **Bacteriophages:** isolated on bacterial lawns.

**Purification of viruses:** clarification by low-speed centrifugation → concentration by ultracentrifugation, PEG precipitation or ultrafiltration → density-gradient centrifugation (sucrose rate-zonal; CsCl isopycnic) → chromatography (ion exchange, size exclusion, affinity) → check purity (EM, SDS-PAGE, infectivity/particle ratio).

**Quantification:** plaque assay (PFU/mL), TCID₅₀ (50% tissue culture infective dose), haemagglutination titre, qPCR (genome copies), electron microscopy particle counts.

### Solved examples

**Example 1: Plaque assay titre.** A virus stock is diluted 10⁻⁶ and 0.1 mL is plated; 45 plaques appear. Titre = plaques / (dilution × volume) = 45 / (10⁻⁶ × 0.1) = **4.5 × 10⁸ PFU/mL**.

**Example 2: Multiplicity of infection (MOI).** 2 × 10⁶ cells are infected with 10⁷ PFU. MOI = 10⁷ / (2 × 10⁶) = **5**. By the Poisson distribution, the fraction of cells infected = 1 − e^(−MOI) = 1 − e^(−5) ≈ **99.3%**. At MOI = 1, only 1 − e^(−1) = 63.2% of cells are infected.

**Example 3: Dilution to plate.** You want about 50 plaques per plate (0.1 mL plated) from a 5 × 10⁷ PFU/mL stock. Needed concentration = 50/0.1 = 500 PFU/mL; dilution = 500 / (5 × 10⁷) = **10⁻⁵**.

### Key points to remember

- Ivanovsky (1892) and Beijerinck (1898) discovered viruses with TMV; Stanley crystallized TMV (1935).
- Symmetry: helical, icosahedral, complex; enveloped or naked.
- Genome: DNA or RNA, ss or ds, linear or circular, segmented, + or − sense.
- Replication: attachment → entry → uncoating → biosynthesis → assembly → release.
- Isolation: cell culture, eggs, animals; purification: centrifugation, density gradient; titre in PFU/mL.

## Unit 2: Virus-Host Interactions and Diagnosis

### 2-mark definitions

- **Lytic infection:** infection ending in host cell lysis and release of progeny virions.
- **Persistent infection:** long-term infection in which the virus continues to be produced at low levels without killing the host cell quickly (hepatitis B).
- **Latent infection:** the viral genome remains in the cell without producing virions, and can reactivate later (herpes simplex in neurons, varicella-zoster causing shingles).
- **Transformation:** conversion of a normal cell into a cancer-like cell by a virus (oncogenic viruses: HPV, HBV, HCV, EBV, HTLV-1, KSHV).
- **Interferons (IFNs):** cytokines produced by infected cells that induce an antiviral state in neighbouring cells (type I: IFN-α, IFN-β; type II: IFN-γ; type III: IFN-λ).
- **Antiviral state:** the condition of a cell exposed to interferon in which hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) block virus replication.
- **Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs):** host sensors that detect viral molecules (TLR3, TLR7/8, TLR9, RIG-I, MDA5, cGAS-STING).
- **RT-PCR:** reverse transcription followed by polymerase chain reaction; detects viral RNA; real-time RT-PCR reports a cycle threshold (Ct).
- **Rapid antigen test (RAT):** a lateral-flow immunoassay that detects viral proteins in a sample in 15–30 minutes.
- **Ct value:** the PCR cycle at which fluorescence crosses the threshold; a lower Ct means more viral nucleic acid.

### Core concepts

**Effects of virus infection on host cells**
- **Cytopathic effects:** cell rounding and detachment, lysis, syncytia (cell fusion: RSV, measles, HIV), inclusion bodies (Negri bodies in rabies, owl's eye in CMV, Guarnieri bodies in poxviruses), vacuolation.
- **Biochemical effects:** shutdown of host protein synthesis (poliovirus cleaves eIF4G), host DNA/RNA synthesis inhibition, cytoskeleton changes, membrane changes.
- **Apoptosis** (programmed cell death) or its inhibition.
- **Transformation** and cancer (HPV E6/E7 inactivate p53 and Rb).
- **Effects on the organism:** inapparent (subclinical), acute, persistent, latent, slow (subacute sclerosing panencephalitis after measles) infections; immunopathology.

**Innate antiviral defence: interferon pathway**
1. **Detection:** viral RNA/DNA sensed by TLRs (in endosomes) and RIG-I/MDA5 (cytoplasmic RNA) or cGAS-STING (cytoplasmic DNA).
2. **Signalling:** adaptors (MAVS, TRIF, MyD88) activate kinases → transcription factors IRF3, IRF7, NF-κB.
3. **IFN production:** IFN-β and IFN-α secreted.
4. **IFN signalling:** IFN binds IFNAR on neighbouring cells → JAK1/TYK2 → STAT1/STAT2 + IRF9 (ISGF3) → ISRE in DNA → ISGs.
5. **ISG effectors:** PKR (phosphorylates eIF2α, stops translation), OAS/RNase L (degrades RNA), Mx proteins (block influenza), IFITMs (block entry), tetherin (blocks release), APOBEC3 (hypermutates HIV), ISG15.
- Also NK cells, complement, inflammation; then adaptive immunity (antibodies, cytotoxic T cells).

**Viral counter-mechanisms (immune evasion)**

| Strategy | Example |
|---|---|
| Block IFN induction | Influenza NS1 binds RNA and RIG-I/TRIM25; HCV NS3/4A protease cleaves MAVS |
| Block IFN signalling | Paramyxovirus V proteins degrade STATs |
| Block ISG action | Adenovirus VA RNA inhibits PKR; vaccinia E3L binds dsRNA |
| Hide from T cells | HIV Nef and herpes proteins down-regulate MHC-I |
| Antigenic variation | Influenza drift and shift; HIV high mutation rate |
| Latency | Herpesviruses hide in neurons or B cells |
| Decoy receptors, cytokine mimics | Poxvirus soluble IFN receptors; EBV IL-10 homologue |
| Inhibit apoptosis | Viral Bcl-2 homologues |
| Infect immune cells | HIV destroys CD4⁺ T cells |

**Diagnostics**

| Method | Detects | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real-time RT-PCR | Viral RNA | Gold standard; very sensitive and specific; quantitative (Ct) | Lab, trained staff, 2–6 h, costly |
| Rapid antigen test | Viral protein | Fast (15–30 min), cheap, point of care | Less sensitive, misses low viral loads |
| ELISA / serology | Antibodies IgM (recent) and IgG (past) | Exposure history, vaccine response | Window period; cannot detect early infection |
| Virus isolation in culture | Live virus | Proves infectivity | Slow (days), biosafety |
| Electron microscopy | Particles by morphology | Rapid identification of unknowns | Insensitive, costly |
| LAMP, CRISPR-based tests | Nucleic acid (isothermal) | Faster, simpler than PCR | Newer, validation |
| Next-generation sequencing | Whole genomes | Variants, new viruses | Costly, bioinformatics |

**RT-PCR steps:** sample (nasopharyngeal swab in viral transport medium) → RNA extraction → reverse transcription (cDNA) → PCR amplification with primers and fluorescent probes (TaqMan) for viral genes (e.g. SARS-CoV-2 E, N, RdRp/ORF1ab) and a human internal control (RNase P) → Ct read-out (e.g. Ct ≤ 35 positive). Each cycle doubles the target, so a difference of 3.3 cycles ≈ 10-fold in template.

**Rapid antigen test (lateral flow):** sample → conjugate pad (gold-labelled antibody binds antigen) → nitrocellulose strip with test line (capture antibody) and control line → coloured lines appear.

### Solved examples

**Example 1: Ct difference.** Patient A has Ct 25 and patient B Ct 30 for the same gene. A has 2^(30 − 25) = 2⁵ = **32 times** more viral RNA.

**Example 2: Predictive values of a rapid antigen test.** Sensitivity 80%, specificity 98%, prevalence 5%, 1,000 people tested.
- Infected = 50: true positives = 40, false negatives = 10.
- Not infected = 950: true negatives = 931, false positives = 19.
- **PPV = 40/(40 + 19) = 67.8%**; **NPV = 931/(931 + 10) = 98.9%**.
- So a positive RAT in a low-prevalence setting should be confirmed by RT-PCR.

**Example 3: Ten-fold rule.** If a standard curve has a slope of −3.32 cycles per log₁₀ (100% efficiency), a sample with Ct 6.64 cycles lower than another has 10² = **100 times** more template.

### Key points to remember

- Infection types: lytic, persistent, latent, transforming.
- IFN pathway: PRR → MAVS/TRIF → IRF3/7 → IFN-β → IFNAR → JAK-STAT (ISGF3) → ISGs (PKR, OAS, Mx).
- Evasion: block IFN, hide from T cells, antigenic variation, latency.
- RT-PCR = gold standard (Ct, 2^ΔCt); RAT = fast but less sensitive; serology = past exposure.

### Important questions

1. Explain the effects of viral infection on host cells and organisms.
2. Explain the interferon-induced antiviral state with a diagram.
3. Explain the counter-mechanisms used by viruses to evade the host's immune response.
4. Explain the principle and steps of RT-PCR for viral diagnosis.
5. Compare RT-PCR, rapid antigen test and serology.

## Unit 3: Classification, Positive-Strand RNA and DNA Viruses

### 2-mark definitions

- **ICTV:** the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses, which classifies and names viruses (realm, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species; family names end in -viridae, genus in -virus).
- **Baltimore classification:** grouping of viruses into seven classes by genome type and the route used to make mRNA (David Baltimore, 1971).
- **Positive-sense (+) RNA:** genome RNA with the same sense as mRNA; can be translated directly and is infectious by itself.
- **Polyprotein:** a long protein translated from one ORF and cut by viral proteases into functional proteins (picornaviruses, flaviviruses, coronaviruses).
- **IRES (internal ribosome entry site):** an RNA structure allowing cap-independent translation (picornaviruses, HCV).
- **Arbovirus:** an arthropod-borne virus transmitted by mosquitoes, ticks or sandflies (dengue, Zika, JE, chikungunya).
- **ACE2:** angiotensin-converting enzyme 2, the human cell receptor used by SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins.
- **Episome:** a viral DNA genome maintained as an extra-chromosomal circle in the nucleus (HPV, EBV, HBV cccDNA).
- **Oncogenic virus:** a virus that can cause cancer (HPV → cervical cancer; HBV/HCV → liver cancer; EBV → Burkitt lymphoma).
- **Latency (herpesviruses):** the viral genome persists without virion production and can reactivate (HSV in sensory ganglia; VZV → shingles).

### Core concepts

**Baltimore classification**

| Class | Genome | mRNA route | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | dsDNA | Host RNA pol II transcribes | Herpes, adeno, pox, papilloma |
| II | ssDNA | Converted to dsDNA, then transcribed | Parvovirus B19, circovirus |
| III | dsRNA | Viral RdRp transcribes | Rotavirus (reo) |
| IV | +ssRNA | Genome acts as mRNA | Polio, dengue, SARS-CoV-2, HCV |
| V | −ssRNA | Viral RdRp copies to + mRNA | Influenza, measles, rabies, Ebola |
| VI | ssRNA-RT | RNA → DNA by reverse transcriptase, integrates | HIV, HTLV |
| VII | dsDNA-RT | Replicates via RNA intermediate | Hepatitis B virus |

**Nomenclature rules (ICTV):** order -virales, family -viridae, subfamily -virinae, genus -virus; species names now binomial (e.g. *Betacoronavirus pandemicum* is the SARS-CoV-2 species since 2023); vernacular names (virus names) are different from species names.

**Picornaviridae ("pico" = small RNA)**
- ~30 nm, non-enveloped icosahedral; +ssRNA ~7.5 kb with VPg (protein) at the 5′ end and poly-A at 3′.
- Replication in cytoplasm: IRES-driven translation of one polyprotein → cleaved by 2A, 3C proteases → RdRp (3D) makes − strand then + strands → assembly → lysis.
- Members: poliovirus (paralysis; OPV/IPV), rhinovirus (common cold), hepatitis A virus, coxsackie and enteroviruses (hand-foot-mouth disease), foot-and-mouth disease virus.

**Flaviviridae**
- ~50 nm, enveloped, icosahedral; +ssRNA ~11 kb, capped, single ORF → polyprotein (structural C, prM, E; non-structural NS1–NS5) cleaved by host and viral NS2B-NS3 protease; NS5 = RdRp and methyltransferase.
- Entry by receptor-mediated endocytosis and low-pH fusion via E protein; assembly in ER; maturation by furin cleavage of prM.
- Members: dengue (4 serotypes; antibody-dependent enhancement; Aedes aegypti), Zika (microcephaly), Japanese encephalitis (Culex; JE vaccine), yellow fever (17D live vaccine), West Nile; hepatitis C virus (genus Hepacivirus; chronic hepatitis; cured by direct-acting antivirals like sofosbuvir).

**Coronaviridae (SARS-CoV-2)**
- ~100–120 nm, enveloped, crown-like spikes; +ssRNA ~30 kb (among the largest RNA genomes), capped and poly-A.
- Structural proteins: **S** (spike: S1 binds ACE2 via RBD; S2 fusion; primed by furin and TMPRSS2), **E** (envelope), **M** (membrane), **N** (nucleocapsid).
- Replication: ORF1a/1ab translated into polyproteins pp1a/pp1ab → cleaved by 3CL^pro (Mpro) and PL^pro into 16 nsps → replication-transcription complex in double-membrane vesicles (nsp12 RdRp, nsp14 proofreading exonuclease) → nested subgenomic mRNAs for structural proteins → assembly in ERGIC → exocytosis.
- Diseases: SARS (2002–03), MERS (2012), COVID-19 (2019–); seasonal HCoVs cause colds. Variants (Alpha, Delta, Omicron) arose by spike mutations.

**Small DNA viruses**

| Family | Genome | Features | Disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parvoviridae | ssDNA ~5 kb, linear | ~20 nm, non-enveloped; needs dividing cells (S phase) or helper virus (AAV) | B19: fifth disease, aplastic crisis; AAV used as gene therapy vector |
| Papillomaviridae | dsDNA ~8 kb, circular | Non-enveloped; infects basal epithelium; E6 degrades p53, E7 inactivates Rb | Warts; HPV 16, 18 → cervical cancer (HPV vaccine) |
| Polyomaviridae | dsDNA ~5 kb, circular | Large T antigen binds p53 and Rb | JC virus (PML), BK virus (kidney), SV40 |

**Large DNA viruses**

| Family | Genome | Features | Disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Herpesviridae | dsDNA 125–240 kb, linear | Enveloped; replicate in nucleus; immediate-early, early, late genes; **latency** | HSV-1/2 (cold sores, genital herpes), VZV (chickenpox, shingles), CMV, EBV (mononucleosis, lymphoma), HHV-8 (Kaposi sarcoma) |
| Poxviridae | dsDNA 130–375 kb | Largest animal viruses; brick-shaped; replicate in **cytoplasm** with own RNA polymerase | Smallpox (eradicated 1980), mpox, vaccinia (vaccine) |
| Adenoviridae | dsDNA 26–45 kb | Non-enveloped icosahedral with fibres | Respiratory and eye infections; vectors for vaccines (ChAdOx1) and gene therapy |

**Miscellaneous agents:** hepatitis B virus (Hepadnaviridae, partly dsDNA, replicates by reverse transcription, cccDNA in nucleus); hepatitis D (satellite needing HBV); viroids; prions; giant viruses (Mimivirus); bacteriophages (lytic and lysogenic cycles).

### Solved examples

**Example 1: Classify by Baltimore.** A virus with a negative-sense, segmented RNA genome that carries its own RdRp → **class V** (e.g. influenza). A virus with dsDNA that replicates through an RNA intermediate → **class VII** (HBV).

**Example 2: Genome coding capacity.** An average protein of 300 amino acids needs 900 nucleotides. A 30 kb coronavirus genome could code roughly 30,000/900 ≈ **33 proteins** (it actually encodes about 29); a 7.5 kb picornavirus about 8 (it makes ~11 mature proteins from one polyprotein by cleavage).

**Example 3: Dengue serotypes.** A person infected first with DENV-2 later catches DENV-3. Non-neutralizing cross-reactive antibodies can help the virus enter Fc-receptor cells (**antibody-dependent enhancement**), raising the risk of severe dengue; this is why dengue vaccines must protect against all four serotypes.

### Key points to remember

- Baltimore I–VII by genome and mRNA route; ICTV ranks and suffixes.
- Picorna: small, naked, +RNA, VPg, IRES, polyprotein (polio, rhino, HAV).
- Flavi: enveloped +RNA, polyprotein, NS3 protease, NS5 RdRp (dengue, Zika, JE, HCV).
- Corona: ~30 kb +RNA, spike-ACE2, TMPRSS2, Mpro, proofreading nsp14, subgenomic mRNAs.
- Small DNA: parvo (ssDNA), papilloma (E6/E7), polyoma; large DNA: herpes (latency), pox (cytoplasm), adeno (vectors).

## Unit 4: Negative-Strand RNA, dsRNA Viruses and Retroviruses

### 2-mark definitions

- **Negative-sense (−) RNA virus:** a virus whose genome is complementary to mRNA; it must carry its own RNA-dependent RNA polymerase into the cell.
- **Segmented genome:** a genome divided into separate RNA molecules (influenza 8 segments; rotavirus 11 segments).
- **Antigenic drift:** small, gradual changes in influenza HA and NA due to point mutations, causing seasonal epidemics.
- **Antigenic shift:** a sudden major change in influenza A HA and/or NA subtype by reassortment of gene segments between strains (e.g. human and avian), which can cause pandemics.
- **Haemagglutinin (HA):** influenza surface glycoprotein that binds sialic acid receptors and mediates fusion.
- **Neuraminidase (NA):** influenza enzyme that cleaves sialic acid to release new virions; target of oseltamivir.
- **Retrovirus:** an RNA virus that uses reverse transcriptase to make a DNA copy of its genome, which integrates into the host chromosome as a provirus.
- **Reverse transcriptase (RT):** an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase (with RNase H activity) that converts viral RNA into dsDNA.
- **Provirus:** retroviral DNA integrated into the host genome.
- **AIDS:** acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, the late stage of HIV infection with CD4⁺ T cells below 200 per µL or AIDS-defining illnesses.

### Core concepts

**Common features of −ssRNA viruses:** enveloped; helical nucleocapsid (RNA + N protein); virion carries RdRp (L protein + P); first event is transcription of mRNAs; later replication through a full-length + antigenome.

**Paramyxoviridae**
- Non-segmented −ssRNA (~15 kb); envelope with attachment protein (HN, H or G) and **F (fusion) protein**, which fuses at neutral pH at the cell surface → syncytia.
- Replication entirely in the cytoplasm; gene order 3′-N-P-M-F-HN-L-5′; transcription gradient (more N than L).
- Members: measles (MMR vaccine; Koplik spots; SSPE), mumps, parainfluenza, **Nipah virus** (henipavirus; bats → humans; outbreaks in Kerala since 2018; high fatality). (RSV is now in Pneumoviridae.)

**Orthomyxoviridae (influenza)**
- Enveloped, pleomorphic; 8 −ssRNA segments (influenza A and B) coding PB2, PB1, PA (polymerase), HA, NP, NA, M1/M2, NS1/NEP.
- Replication in the **nucleus**: entry by endocytosis, M2 ion channel acidifies the core, HA-mediated fusion, vRNPs enter nucleus; **cap-snatching** (PB2 binds host caps, PA cleaves them) primes viral mRNA synthesis; assembly at the plasma membrane; NA releases virions.
- Subtypes by HA (H1–H18) and NA (N1–N11); pandemics 1918 (H1N1), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2), 2009 (H1N1pdm); avian H5N1, H7N9.
- Drift → annual vaccine updates; shift → pandemics. Drugs: oseltamivir, zanamivir (NA inhibitors), baloxavir (PA endonuclease inhibitor), amantadine (M2, now resistant).

**Rhabdoviridae (rabies)**
- Bullet-shaped, enveloped; −ssRNA ~12 kb with 5 genes N, P, M, G, L; G glycoprotein binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptor and others.
- Transmission by bite of infected animals (dogs cause 99% of human cases in India); travels by retrograde axonal transport to the CNS; Negri bodies in neurons; almost 100% fatal once symptoms appear.
- Prevention: wound washing, post-exposure prophylaxis (cell-culture rabies vaccine + rabies immunoglobulin for category III bites), pre-exposure vaccination; dog vaccination ("zero by 30" goal).

**Reoviridae / rotavirus (dsRNA)**
- Non-enveloped, triple-layered icosahedral capsid; 11 dsRNA segments.
- The dsRNA genome stays inside the inner core particle (hiding from PRRs); core RdRp transcribes capped mRNAs extruded through channels; replication in cytoplasmic viroplasms.
- NSP4 acts as an enterotoxin → secretory diarrhoea; leading cause of severe diarrhoea in infants.
- Vaccines: Rotavac (Indian, Bharat Biotech), Rotasiil (Serum Institute), RotaTeq, Rotarix; in India's Universal Immunization Programme.

**Retroviruses and HIV**
- Structure: enveloped ~100 nm; cone-shaped capsid (p24); diploid +ssRNA (~9.7 kb); enzymes RT, integrase, protease; envelope gp120 (SU) + gp41 (TM).
- Genes: **gag** (structural: MA, CA, NC), **pol** (protease, RT, integrase), **env** (gp160 → gp120 + gp41); accessory/regulatory: tat, rev, nef, vif, vpr, vpu.
- **Life cycle (draw it):**
  1. gp120 binds CD4 and co-receptor CCR5 or CXCR4.
  2. gp41 mediates membrane fusion; capsid enters.
  3. Reverse transcription: RNA → ssDNA → dsDNA (error-prone → high mutation rate).
  4. Nuclear import and integration by integrase → provirus.
  5. Transcription by host RNA pol II (Tat boosts; Rev exports unspliced RNA).
  6. Translation of Gag and Gag-Pol polyproteins; Env processed in ER/Golgi.
  7. Assembly at plasma membrane and budding.
  8. Maturation: viral protease cleaves Gag and Gag-Pol → infectious virion.
- **HIV/AIDS:** transmission by sex, blood, mother-to-child; acute phase (flu-like, high viraemia) → clinical latency (years, CD4 decline) → AIDS (CD4 < 200, opportunistic infections: TB, Pneumocystis, candidiasis, Kaposi sarcoma).
- **Treatment (ART):** combination of NRTIs (tenofovir, lamivudine, zidovudine), NNRTIs (efavirenz, nevirapine), protease inhibitors (lopinavir, darunavir), integrase inhibitors (dolutegravir), entry inhibitors (maraviroc, enfuvirtide). India's first-line: TLD (tenofovir + lamivudine + dolutegravir). "Undetectable = untransmittable".
- Diagnosis: ELISA/rapid antibody tests, Western blot (older), p24 antigen, viral load by RT-PCR, CD4 count.

### Solved examples

**Example 1: Influenza reassortment.** A pig cell co-infected with human H3N2 and avian H5N1 can produce progeny with any combination of the 8 segments: 2⁸ = **256** possible genotypes, including new HA/NA combinations like H5N2 or H3N1 (antigenic shift).

**Example 2: HIV mutation rate.** HIV RT makes about 3 × 10⁻⁵ errors per nucleotide per cycle; with a 9,700-nt genome, each new genome carries about 9,700 × 3 × 10⁻⁵ ≈ **0.3 mutations**, so about one in three progeny carries a new mutation. With billions of virions a day, every single drug-resistance mutation arises daily → need for **combination therapy**.

**Example 3: CD4 count.** A patient has 1,800 lymphocytes/µL with 10% CD4⁺ cells → CD4 = **180/µL**, below 200 → AIDS-defining by count; needs ART and prophylaxis against opportunistic infections.

### Key points to remember

- −RNA viruses carry RdRp; transcription first.
- Paramyxo: F protein, syncytia, cytoplasmic; measles, mumps, Nipah.
- Influenza: 8 segments, nuclear replication, cap-snatching, HA/NA, drift vs shift, NA inhibitors.
- Rabies: bullet shape, N-P-M-G-L, retrograde axonal transport, Negri bodies, PEP.
- Rotavirus: 11 dsRNA segments, triple capsid, NSP4 enterotoxin, Rotavac.
- HIV: gag-pol-env, CD4 + CCR5/CXCR4, RT, integrase, protease; ART combination.

3. Explain rabies virus: structure, pathogenesis and prevention.

## Unit 5: Vaccines, Antivirals and RNA-Based Therapies

### 2-mark definitions

- **Vaccine:** a biological preparation that trains the immune system to recognize a pathogen and protect against future infection, without causing the disease.
- **Live attenuated vaccine:** a weakened live virus that replicates without causing disease (OPV, MMR, yellow fever 17D, rotavirus, varicella).
- **Inactivated (killed) vaccine:** virus killed by heat or chemicals (formaldehyde, β-propiolactone) (IPV, rabies cell-culture vaccine, Covaxin).
- **Subunit vaccine:** contains only purified or recombinant viral proteins (hepatitis B HBsAg vaccine; HPV virus-like particles).
- **mRNA vaccine:** lipid-nanoparticle-delivered mRNA encoding a viral antigen that host cells translate (COVID-19 vaccines by Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna).
- **Adjuvant:** a substance added to a vaccine to strengthen and direct the immune response (alum, MF59, AS01, AS04, CpG).
- **Antiviral drug:** a drug that inhibits a specific step of the virus life cycle with acceptable toxicity to host cells.
- **Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO):** a short single-stranded DNA/RNA analogue complementary to a target mRNA that blocks its translation or triggers its degradation (RNase H).
- **siRNA (small interfering RNA):** a 21–23 nt double-stranded RNA that guides the RISC complex (Argonaute) to cleave a complementary mRNA.
- **Ribozyme:** a catalytic RNA molecule that cleaves specific RNA sequences (hammerhead, hairpin ribozymes).

### Core concepts

**Types of viral vaccines**

| Type | Examples | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Live attenuated | OPV, MMR, yellow fever, varicella, rotavirus, nasal influenza | Strong, long-lasting cellular and humoral immunity; often one dose; cheap | Reversion risk (vaccine-derived polio); not for immunocompromised or pregnant; cold chain |
| Inactivated | IPV, rabies, hepatitis A, Covaxin, injectable flu | Safe, no reversion | Weaker, needs boosters and adjuvants |
| Subunit / recombinant protein | Hepatitis B, Novavax, Corbevax (India) | Very safe, defined | Needs adjuvant, boosters |
| Virus-like particles (VLPs) | HPV (Gardasil, Cervavac — India's indigenous HPV vaccine) | Highly immunogenic, non-infectious | Complex production |
| Viral vector | Covishield (ChAdOx1), Sputnik V, Ebola rVSV-ZEBOV | Strong T-cell response | Pre-existing immunity to vector |
| mRNA | Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna, India's Gemcovac | Rapid design and manufacture | Cold chain (lipid nanoparticles), cost |
| DNA | ZyCoV-D (India, 2021: world's first DNA vaccine approved for humans) | Stable, easy to make | Lower immunogenicity, needle-free injector |

**Adjuvants:** aluminium salts (alum: depot effect, inflammasome activation); oil-in-water emulsions (MF59, AS03); AS01 (MPL + QS-21 in liposomes; shingles and malaria vaccines); AS04 (MPL + alum; HPV); TLR agonists (CpG 1018 in hepatitis B vaccine; Algel-IMDG in Covaxin). Functions: enhance antigen uptake, activate innate immunity, prolong antigen exposure, reduce dose, direct Th1/Th2 response.

**Vaccine manufacturing**
1. Master and working seed lots (virus) and cell banks (Vero, MRC-5) or eggs.
2. Upstream: virus propagation in bioreactors or eggs, or recombinant protein expression (yeast, insect, CHO cells), or mRNA in vitro transcription.
3. Downstream: harvest, clarification, purification (chromatography, ultrafiltration), inactivation (if inactivated vaccine).
4. Formulation with adjuvant, stabilizers, preservatives; fill-finish in vials; lyophilization if needed.
5. Packaging, cold chain (2–8 °C; −20/−70 °C for some mRNA vaccines).

**Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC):** Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP); tests for identity, **potency** (antigen content, animal or cell-based assays), **sterility**, purity (host-cell protein and DNA limits), **safety** (abnormal toxicity, absence of adventitious agents, complete inactivation tests), endotoxin (LAL test), pyrogens, stability and shelf-life, adjuvant content, pH, appearance. Lot release by the national control laboratory (Central Drugs Laboratory, Kasauli) after CDSCO approval; WHO prequalification for global supply; vaccine vial monitors; post-marketing surveillance (AEFI).

**Antivirals: targets in the life cycle**

| Step | Drug (virus) |
|---|---|
| Attachment/entry | Maraviroc (HIV CCR5), enfuvirtide (HIV gp41 fusion), palivizumab and nirsevimab (RSV antibodies) |
| Uncoating | Amantadine (influenza M2; resistance now common) |
| Genome replication: nucleoside/nucleotide analogues | Acyclovir (HSV/VZV; activated by viral thymidine kinase → selective), ganciclovir (CMV), zidovudine, lamivudine, tenofovir (HIV/HBV), sofosbuvir (HCV NS5B), remdesivir (SARS-CoV-2 RdRp), molnupiravir (lethal mutagenesis), favipiravir |
| Non-nucleoside RT inhibitors | Nevirapine, efavirenz (HIV) |
| Integration | Raltegravir, dolutegravir (HIV) |
| Cap-snatching | Baloxavir (influenza PA) |
| Polyprotein processing | Lopinavir, darunavir (HIV protease); nirmatrelvir (SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, with ritonavir as Paxlovid); glecaprevir (HCV NS3/4A) |
| Release | Oseltamivir, zanamivir (influenza neuraminidase) |
| Immune modulation | Interferon-α (HBV, HCV earlier), pegylated interferon |

**Interferons as drugs:** recombinant IFN-α (and pegylated IFN-α with longer half-life) for chronic hepatitis B and earlier HCV; IFN-β for multiple sclerosis; side effects: flu-like symptoms, depression, bone marrow suppression.

**Screening of antivirals:** target-based (enzyme assays on RT, protease, polymerase) and cell-based (CPE inhibition, plaque reduction, reporter viruses) high-throughput screening; measure **EC₅₀** (effective concentration) and **CC₅₀** (cytotoxic concentration); **selectivity index SI = CC₅₀/EC₅₀** (higher is safer, > 10 desired); then animal models and clinical trials (phases I–III).

**Antiviral resistance:** arises from high mutation rates and selection under drug pressure: examples: HIV M184V (lamivudine), K103N (NNRTIs); influenza H275Y (oseltamivir), S31N (amantadine); HSV thymidine kinase mutants (acyclovir). Prevention: combination therapy, adherence, high-barrier drugs, resistance testing.

**Antisense, siRNA and ribozymes**

| Approach | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Antisense oligonucleotides | Bind target mRNA by base pairing → block translation or RNase H cleavage; chemically modified (phosphorothioate, 2′-O-methyl) for stability | Fomivirsen (CMV retinitis, first approved antisense drug, 1998) |
| siRNA (RNA interference) | dsRNA loaded into RISC; guide strand directs Argonaute-2 to cleave complementary viral mRNA | Approved for genetic diseases (patisiran); experimental against HBV, RSV, SARS-CoV-2 |
| Ribozymes | Catalytic RNA that base-pairs with and cleaves target RNA (hammerhead, hairpin) | Tested in HIV gene therapy |

Challenges: delivery into cells (lipid nanoparticles, GalNAc conjugates for liver), stability against nucleases, off-target effects, innate immune activation, viral escape mutations (use several targets).

### Solved examples

**Example 1: Herd immunity threshold.** For R₀ = 3, the threshold = 1 − 1/R₀ = **66.7%** of the population must be immune. With a vaccine of 90% efficacy, coverage needed = 0.667/0.9 = **74%**. For measles (R₀ ≈ 15): 1 − 1/15 = 93.3% immune; with 95% efficacy, coverage ≈ 98%.

**Example 2: Selectivity index.** A compound has EC₅₀ = 0.5 µM and CC₅₀ = 200 µM. SI = 200/0.5 = **400** → highly selective, a good lead. Another with EC₅₀ = 10 µM, CC₅₀ = 50 µM has SI = 5 → too toxic.

**Example 3: Vaccine efficacy.** In a trial, 100 of 10,000 placebo recipients and 10 of 10,000 vaccinees get the disease. Efficacy = (1 − 0.001/0.01) × 100 = **90%**.

### Key points to remember

- Vaccine types: live, inactivated, subunit, VLP, vector, mRNA, DNA (Indian examples: Covaxin, Covishield, ZyCoV-D, Corbevax, Cervavac, Rotavac).
- Adjuvants: alum, MF59, AS01, AS04, CpG.
- Manufacturing: seed lot → upstream → downstream → formulation → fill-finish; QC: identity, potency, sterility, purity, safety, endotoxin, stability; CDL Kasauli lot release.
- Antivirals by life-cycle step; acyclovir selective via viral TK; oseltamivir NA; remdesivir RdRp.
- SI = CC₅₀/EC₅₀; resistance → combination therapy.
- Antisense (fomivirsen), siRNA (RISC), ribozymes (catalytic RNA).

### Important questions

1. Classify viral vaccines with examples, advantages and limitations.
2. Explain the role of adjuvants and modern vaccine technologies (mRNA, vector, DNA).
3. Explain vaccine manufacturing and QA/QC.
4. Explain the mechanisms of antiviral drugs, interferons, antiviral screening and resistance.
5. Explain antisense RNA, siRNA and ribozymes as antiviral strategies.
